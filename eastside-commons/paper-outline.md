# The Commons as Infrastructure: Site Geometry, Cooperative Economics, and Siege-Grade Development at Military Circle

## Working paper — Eastside Commons Planning Project
### Jason Edelman · Claude Sonnet 4.6 · May 2026

---

## Abstract

We present a planning framework for the redevelopment of the 73-acre Military Circle site in Norfolk, Virginia as a community land trust. The framework departs from conventional redevelopment practice in three respects: (1) it treats green infrastructure as the primary site constraint rather than a residual use, (2) it incorporates light-industrial and live/work uses to enable community-owned construction capacity, and (3) it sequences development from existing structures outward using a self-financing revolving fund designed to operate without federal program continuity. We argue that the geometry of the development plan is not separable from its economic and political structure — that the site layout *enacts* the argument for community ownership rather than merely illustrating it. We develop this through the Jacobs/North End parallel, the Mondragón sequencing model, and the Rojava institutional resilience framework, and we propose a minimum viable activation threshold — what we call Phase 0 — based on the existing mall structure, Virginia's 2024 off-site construction standards, and a 100-stall indoor market model.

## I. The geometry problem is the argument

Standard redevelopment planning treats site geometry as a technical output — the form that results from applying uses, densities, setbacks, and financial models to a parcel. We argue this is backwards. The geometry should *precede* and *constrain* the model, because the spatial relationships between uses are the mechanism by which the economic argument is made or not made.

At Military Circle, four geometric principles follow directly from the siege-grade CLT model:

**1. Green infrastructure is first, not last.**
In conventional development, green space is what's left after buildable area is determined. At Military Circle, the sponge park, wetland buffer, and internal green network should be established first — because they are the slowest to build, the hardest to establish once disturbed, and the first thing a hostile administration or developer would sacrifice under pressure. Green infrastructure established early becomes a legal and ecological fact that subsequent development must accommodate, not a plan element that subsequent pressures can eliminate.

**2. The internal street network is the governance structure made physical.**
Jacobs's observation about the North End was that short blocks, dense intersections, and mixed-use adjacencies produced the social friction that generated community cohesion. At Military Circle, the internal circulation system should be designed to maximize intersection between different use types — residential with workshop, market with green, trades with civic — because accidental encounter is what produces the social capital the CLT needs to be self-governing. A development whose internal streets segregate uses by income or function is a development whose governance will be captured by whoever controls the most expensive zone.

**3. Phase 0 uses existing structure, not new construction.**
The main mall building (935,000 sq ft, steel frame, climate-controlled) is standing. Ross Dress for Less leases through 2036. Sentara owns the former JCPenney box. Rather than treating these tenants as obstacles to demolition, the framework treats them as anchors for Phase 0 activation. The mall is already the indoor market. The Sentara box is already the health infrastructure node. Development radiates outward from existing use rather than waiting for a clean slate.

**4. Workshop and depot uses are not peripheral — they are central.**
In most mixed-use developments, light-industrial uses are pushed to the edge, buffered from residential. The Mondragón model suggests the opposite: the technical school and the fabrication shop are the seed from which everything else grows. At Military Circle, the trades corridor — workshop bays, equipment depot, materials yard, fabrication space — should be adjacent to the transit node (the future Tide stop) and the technical school, not pushed to the I-264 edge. The tradespeople who build the subsequent phases live on site, work on site, and supply from on site. The development is its own construction ecosystem.

## II. What's there now and what we can use

**Existing structures (as of mid-2025):**
- Main mall building: 935,000 sq ft, steel frame, skylights, HVAC. Ross Dress for Less (lease through 2036, veto power over demolition under old operating agreement). Empty concourse, food court, former anchor boxes.
- Sentara Health: Former JCPenney box, Sentara-owned (not EDA tenant). Active medical use. This is an anchor — healthcare adjacency on the highest ground in a flooding city is a permanent asset.
- Parking: ~7,000 spaces across the site, mostly surface. Currently leasing some to Boyd Gaming for casino construction parking (~$65k/year). This is Phase 0 revenue.
- Out-parcels: All demolished (China Garden, Martin's Café, The Military Circle Shoppes, Piccadilly Cafeteria, Palace Shops, Swagath Plaza). These are clear, mostly flat, and available for Phase 0 activation.
- Sheraton hotel: Status unclear post-demolition of outparcels — needs verification.
- Infrastructure: Existing utility connections, storm drains, road access from Va. Beach Blvd, Military Highway, Tidewater Drive, and the I-264 frontage road.

**The stall market argument:**
The main mall's empty concourse is already a European-style indoor market. It has skylights, climate control, electrical infrastructure, loading docks, and parking. It requires no construction — only activation. Sectioning off 50,000-100,000 sq ft of the empty concourse into stall bays and filling them with 80-100 vendors generates:
- Revenue: 100 stalls × $300/month = $30,000/month
- Operating costs for the activated section: ~$12-15,000/month (utilities, security, cleaning)
- Net: ~$15,000/month surplus toward the revolving fund
- Timeline to activate: 60-90 days from decision

Ross is the constraint, not the obstacle. Ross generates rent income. The old operating agreement that gives Ross demolition veto is a structural fact — but it doesn't prevent activating the empty space. The CLT negotiates a side-agreement with Ross that separates the stall market activation from the eventual demolition timeline. Ross stays, the market opens around them.

**The Sentara relationship:**
Sentara owning the former JCPenney box on the highest ground in a flooding city is not a problem. It's an anchor partner. Healthcare on the highest ground in Hampton Roads is infrastructure for the whole city. The CLT relationship with Sentara is: we build the housing and cooperative economy around the health node you've already established. The sponge park and wetland network protect the building's basement infrastructure. The trades program produces HVAC and electrical workers who maintain Sentara's systems. This is a mutual interest, not a lease negotiation.

## III. Zoning — the live/work and light industrial question

Norfolk's current C-R (Regional Commercial) zoning on the site would require a Planned Development (PD) rezoning regardless of what gets built — the scale of the project requires it. The PD process is the single council vote. The PD master plan can enumerate uses not available in standard districts.

**The live/work question:**
Norfolk's zoning code does not currently have a specific live/work district, but the PD process can create one. Precedent: the 2020 Conditional Use Permit for #1 Space (arts education center with live entertainment) in the former mall confirms that the C-R district allowed arts uses, and the PD can extend this to artist/maker live/work explicitly. The key statute is Va. Code § 15.2-2286, which gives localities broad authority to establish PD districts with custom use lists.

The live/work model for Eastside Commons: a tradespeople's live/work tier where residential units share a parcel boundary (but not a building) with workshop bays. The worker lives in a CLT residential unit; their workshop lease is attached to their ground lease, meaning they can't be displaced from the workshop without also being displaced from their home, and vice versa. This is a single integrated tenure arrangement, not two separate transactions.

**The workshop/depot/fabrication question:**
Under the PD master plan, the following industrial-adjacent uses can be enumerated as permitted:
- Workshop bays (indoor fabrication, 1,500-3,000 sq ft each)
- Equipment depots (open yard storage for construction equipment, vehicles, materials)
- Materials yards (lumber, concrete, HVAC components — sheltered storage)
- Maker spaces / shared fabrication (CNC, welding, wood shop — shared equipment, membership-based access)

These are I-1 (Light Industrial) uses in the standard code. In a PD, they become a named zone within the master plan — call it the Trades Corridor — with specific adjacency rules, operating hours, noise buffers, and access requirements. The trades corridor sits between the residential zones and the I-264 edge: it buffers residential from highway noise while providing the construction logistics capacity the development needs.

**The key insight:**
Development is an opportunity, not a nuisance, when the people who build it live there and own the equipment. A materials depot and equipment yard on site means Phase 2 construction doesn't require importing everything from off-site contractors. The trades graduates from the technical school operate the depot cooperatively. The cooperative charges Phase 2 construction teams for materials and equipment. The surplus goes into the revolving fund. The development is its own supply chain.

## IV. Virginia's modular construction advantage

Virginia became the first state to adopt ICC/MBI Standards 1200 and 1205 for off-site construction, effective January 18, 2024. This is a planning accelerant of the first order.

**What it means practically:**
Factory-built modules are inspected and approved at the factory, not the site. When they arrive at Military Circle, they are pre-certified. The site inspection is a placement and connection verification, not a full code review. This compresses the typical 12-18 month construction permit review cycle significantly.

**The geometry implication:**
If the units are modular, the geometry should be modular. This means:
- Standardized parcel widths that accommodate module dimensions (typically 14-16 ft wide, 60-70 ft long)
- Street grid designed to allow truck access to every parcel without crane lifting over buildings
- Foundation systems pre-designed for module placement — not cast-in-place slabs requiring individual design review
- A Type A and Type B unit standardized for the site, pre-approved by the city before the first parcel is developed

**The cooperative production implication:**
If you're building 500-800 units over 10 years using the same module types, you can establish a small-scale modular fabrication operation on site — or in partnership with an existing Virginia manufacturer. The trades corridor includes a module assembly bay. Workers who will live in the finished units help build them. This is the sweat equity model scaled to modular production.

Virginia Housing's Innovative Demonstrations Program (2019-present) has been funding exactly these kinds of manufacturer-municipality partnerships. This is a state-level program that doesn't flow through federal HUD.

## V. The Mondragón/Rochdale incubation sequence adapted

**What Mondragón actually did (the replicable part):**
1. Technical school first (1943-1956). Education before production. The school built the social trust and technical capacity that made everything else possible.
2. First cooperative (Ulgor, 1956) — five students, manufacturing. Started with what the school graduates could actually make.
3. Caja Laboral (1959) — the cooperative bank. This is the critical step. The bank lent to new cooperatives and required them to meet cooperative governance standards as a condition of lending. It made cooperative formation the path of least resistance for new businesses.
4. Retail cooperatives (Eroski, 1969) — grocery, procurement, distribution. Consumer cooperatives that captured spending that was leaving the community.
5. Research and development (Ikerlan, 1974) — only after the economic base was established.

**What Rochdale actually did (the replicable part):**
28 weavers, £28, a small grocery cooperative. The insight: start with what you already buy. Collective purchasing of groceries, hardware, coal. The surplus from collective purchasing funded the next cooperative. The consumer cooperative preceded the production cooperative.

**The Eastside Commons sequence:**
The Mondragón insight says: school first, then cooperative production.
The Rochdale insight says: collective purchasing first, production later.
These are not in conflict — they're complementary phases.

**Phase 0 (Year 0-2): Activation without construction**
- Stall market in existing mall building (100 stalls, $30k/month revenue)
- Cooperative grocery (collective purchasing, consumer surplus)
- Equipment depot (cooperative ownership of construction tools — rented to any contractor, profits to the CLT)
- Parking revenue from Boyd Gaming and others
- No construction. No new zoning. No permits for new buildings.
- Required: 20-30 committed stallholders, 5-7 organizing committee, $150,000 seed capital, one council vote to charter the CLT (can precede the PD rezoning)
- Goal: Generate enough operating surplus to seed the revolving fund while the PD rezoning moves through the planning process

**Phase 1 (Year 2-4): First construction, modular**
- PD rezoning complete. Revolving fund seeded from Phase 0 surplus plus local foundation investment.
- First residential modules: 60-80 units, pre-certified under ICC/MBI 1200/1205, built by trades graduates using materials from on-site depot
- Technical school opens — first class of 20-30 students
- Cooperative incubator formally established: any new business in Phase 1 gets 24-month rent deferral in exchange for cooperative incorporation and a cooperative development fund contribution
- Caja Laboral analog: the revolving fund begins lending to new cooperatives at below-market rates, requiring cooperative governance as a condition
- Goal: Demonstrate financial viability, produce the first class of trades graduates, establish the cooperative formation pipeline

**Phase 2 (Year 4-8): Scale the pattern**
- Second residential block, larger. Trades corridor fully operational.
- Module production partnership with a Virginia manufacturer — possible on-site assembly
- Cooperative supply chain formalized: lumber, concrete, HVAC, electrical all procured collectively
- Each dollar spent inside the community instead of outside it compounds: target 40% of construction spending captured internally by Year 8
- Tide extension opens (~2027-2028): transit adjacency reprices the corridor; CLT captures the land value increment rather than distributing it to private owners

**Phase 3 (Year 8-15): The Caja Laboral moment**
- Revolving fund large enough to self-finance new phases without external capital
- Cooperative ecosystem: 20-30 worker-owned businesses, all procuring from each other where possible
- Technical school graduates now operating businesses that employ the next class
- The development is its own supply chain for its own construction
- Federal programs used whenever available as acceleration, never as foundation

## VI. The critical mass question — minimum viable community

**What kills community land trusts:**
Not politics. Not zoning. Not financing. Empty streets. If nobody shows up, the whole thing fails regardless of legal structure.

The minimum viable community is not a number of units. It's a number of *daily interactions* — the Jacobs metric. You need enough people doing enough different things in enough different places that the sidewalks feel alive before you've finished building.

**The Phase 0 target:**
- 80-100 active stalls in the market by Month 6
- 300+ weekly visitors to the market by Month 3 (before all stalls are filled — the market needs to be worth visiting before it's worth vending in)
- 20 founding cooperative members by Day 1 (these are the people who show up regardless of whether the market is full)
- The Eastside Civic League as the organizing spine — they are already organized, they have the relationships, they have the standing

**The 20 founding members question:**
Who are they? They are the people who lose the most if this doesn't happen. Not the optimists — the people who have been watching this site sit empty for ten years while their neighborhoods flooded and their neighbors left. The founding members of Rochdale were weavers who had been starving through a wage depression. The founding members of Mondragón were Franco-era Basque nationalists who had no other economic path. The founding members of Eastside Commons are Ward 4 residents who have been watching Downtown Tunnel Vision extract investment from their neighborhood for decades. The motivation is already there. The structure is what's been missing.

**The stall market as recruitment tool:**
The Phase 0 stall market is not just revenue — it's the organizing meeting that never ends. Every stallholder is a daily community conversation. Every customer is a potential founding member of the next cooperative. The market is the social infrastructure that Jacobs identified in the North End: the place where the parallel financial institutions become visible and real to people who didn't know they could exist.

## VII. New constraints for the solver

The additions to the Eastside Commons constraint solver required by this analysis:

**New use types:**
- `MARKET_HALL`: Phase 0 activation of existing mall structure. Constraint: must overlap with MALL_FOOTPRINT. Synergy: RETAIL_GROUND, CIVIC, PLAZA. Priority: 0 (Phase 0, first-placed).
- `WORKSHOP_FABRICATION`: Indoor workshop bays, 1,500-3,000 sq ft. Conflict: RESIDENTIAL (noise buffer required, minimum 50 ft separation). Synergy: LIVE_WORK, DEPOT_YARD, RESEARCH. I-1 equivalent in PD context.
- `DEPOT_YARD`: Open/sheltered yard for equipment and materials storage. Conflict: RESIDENTIAL (minimum 100 ft setback), GREEN_PASSIVE. Synergy: WORKSHOP_FABRICATION, TRANSIT (delivery access). Edge constraint: must have truck-accessible road frontage.
- `LIVE_WORK`: Residential unit + workshop bay as integrated tenure. Constraint: residential above, workshop below or adjacent. Conflict: none (self-contained). Synergy: WORKSHOP_FABRICATION, RETAIL_GROUND, RESIDENTIAL_CLT_OWN.
- `COOP_INCUBATOR`: Business incubation space. Small storefronts (200-600 sq ft), shared equipment, meeting rooms. Synergy: CIVIC, RETAIL_GROUND, MARKET_HALL, WORKSHOP_FABRICATION. Constraint: must be adjacent to CIVIC or MARKET_HALL.

**New constraints:**
- Green infrastructure priority: GREEN_PASSIVE and GREEN_ACTIVE zones receive a large initialization bonus (placed in Tier 1 before residential zones, not after)
- Modular geometry constraint: residential parcel widths must be multiples of 16 ft (module width) with minimum truck access road width of 14 ft on all residential streets
- Trades corridor adjacency: WORKSHOP_FABRICATION and DEPOT_YARD must be adjacent to TRANSIT zone (future Tide stop) and within 400 ft of LIVE_WORK or RESIDENTIAL_CLT_OWN
- Internal street network metric: score for intersection density, not just zone connectivity (Jacobs friction metric)
- Phase 0 constraint: MARKET_HALL must be placed at the existing mall footprint centroid; this is a hard constraint, not a soft score

**New synergies:**
- WORKSHOP_FABRICATION ↔ LIVE_WORK: 5 (highest — the live/work bond)
- COOP_INCUBATOR ↔ MARKET_HALL: 4
- DEPOT_YARD ↔ TRANSIT: 3 (construction logistics node at transit stop)
- GREEN_ACTIVE ↔ LIVE_WORK: 3 (green adjacency raises residential quality)
- RESEARCH ↔ WORKSHOP_FABRICATION: 3 (ODU/NSU partnership on applied trades research)

**New conflicts:**
- DEPOT_YARD ↔ RESIDENTIAL_MARKET: 6 (highest conflict — noise, truck traffic, aesthetics)
- DEPOT_YARD ↔ GREEN_PASSIVE: 5 (ecological buffer required)
- WORKSHOP_FABRICATION ↔ RESIDENTIAL_AFFORDABLE: 4 (noise buffer required, 50 ft minimum)

## VIII. The paper's argument — one sentence

The geometry of a community land trust is not a technical output of financial modeling — it is the structure of the governance argument made spatial: green infrastructure first because it cannot be traded away later, live/work integration because the people who build the neighborhood should own a piece of it, and a Phase 0 market in an existing building because waiting for perfect conditions is how community development dies.
