The situation

Norfolk sits where the Chesapeake Bay meets the Atlantic. The Naval Station, the Port of Virginia, some of the oldest neighborhoods on the East Coast. It's also one of the most flood-vulnerable cities in America — not just from hurricanes, but from the slow, steady combination of sea level rise and land subsidence. The ground is sinking. The water is rising. Both at once.

The flooding is already happening. Not hypothetically. In Chesterfield Heights, in parts of Larchmont, in the low-lying streets near the Elizabeth River, it happens in bad storms. It happens with unusually high tides. It gets worse every decade. The people who live in those neighborhoods are going to need somewhere to go.

"The climate argument and the housing argument are, in Norfolk, the same argument."

— The highest ground should house people, not a failing mall

In 2022, the Norfolk Economic Development Authority acquired the Military Circle Mall site — 73 acres at the I-264/I-64 nexus — for $10. Not $10 million. Ten dollars. The site sits at 8 to 12 feet elevation. Flood Zone X: the lowest flood risk classification. As the neighborhoods around it face rising water, this site becomes more valuable, not less.

Three developers proposed plans — Pharrell Williams, Emmitt Smith, Bruce Thompson. Each wanted to buy or long-lease the land. Each was shelved. The current placeholder: a library and a fitness center. Not a vision. A signal that no one in City Hall has decided what this land is for.

The other way

What if the answer to flooding is ownership?

The standard response to climate displacement is reactive: wait until people are flooded out, then try to rehouse them. The commons argument is different. Build the resilient community before people need it. Use public land — already owned, already flood-safe — to create housing under permanent community control, priced for the people most likely to need it.

$10 What the city paid for 73 acres. Land cost: zero. The hard part is already done.

The plan is called Eastside Commons. The core rule: the city doesn't sell the land. It transfers it to a Community Land Trust that holds it permanently. Buildings go up, people move in, businesses open — but the land never goes to market. No future city council can sell it. No future developer can buy it. Rising land values don't displace anyone because rising land values flow back into the commons.

Because land is typically 25–40% of any development's total cost, removing it from the equation makes permanently affordable housing financially viable without ongoing subsidy. Three federal programs — LIHTC tax credit equity, Virginia Housing construction loans, CDFI impact debt — are specifically designed for this.

The model works. It has a track record.

The Champlain Housing Trust in Burlington, Vermont has been running this model for 30 years. 2,500 permanently affordable homes. Condos that have been resold at affordable prices through five ownership cycles. Not an experiment — a proven system.

What's needed in Norfolk is one council vote: rezone Military Circle and charter a Community Land Trust. A Planned Development rezoning — the same vehicle used for East Beach, Harbor Walk, and Broad Creek — replaces 200 individual permits with one master plan. One vote that could permanently lock 5,118 homes into community ownership.

The Eastside Civic League named the problem. The commons plan names the solution. The land is already owned. The numbers work. The only missing piece is political will.
