The hard questions

Who pays.
Who governs.
Why now.

Who pays? The city contributes land it already owns — no cash, no bond issuance. Federal programs (Low-Income Housing Tax Credits, Virginia Housing construction loans, community development lending) are specifically designed for this structure and cover construction financing. The project generates its own operating income from Year 1. No ongoing city subsidy required.

Who governs it? A Community Land Trust — resident board, majority-elected by tenants. Not the city, not a developer. The Champlain Housing Trust has run this way for over 40 years through multiple hostile administrations. The structure holds because it's designed to.

Won't this concentrate poverty? 40% deeply affordable, 35% workforce ownership, 25% market rate — same land, same block, same governance. Mixed-income by design. The research is consistent: economic mixing produces better outcomes for everyone in the neighborhood.

Why hasn't anyone done this here before? Three private developer proposals have been shelved since the city acquired the land. The city's current placeholder is a library and a fitness center. The commons model hasn't been tried here because no one has formally proposed it yet.

The opportunity

The city of Norfolk owns
the land already.

In 2022, Norfolk's Economic Development Authority acquired the Military Circle Mall site — 73 acres at the I-264/I-64 nexus, in Ward 4, at the highest elevation in a city sinking 4–5mm per year — for a nominal sum. The land has been in public hands ever since.

In most cities, the reason permanently affordable housing is nearly impossible to build is simple: land costs a fortune. Twenty-five to forty percent of any project's total cost is just the ground beneath it. Here, the city already holds the asset. The hardest part — acquiring the land without debt — is already done.

$0 Land acquisition cost under the commons model. The city already owns it. The subsidy already exists. It's called public land — and it belongs to all of us.

Since the acquisition: three developer proposals — Pharrell Williams, Emmitt Smith, Bruce Thompson — each fell apart, for reasons that likely include financing gaps and post-COVID construction cost increases. The city's current placeholder is a library and a fitness center. Not a plan — a signal that no decision has been made.

The Eastside Civic League named the pattern — "Downtown Tunnel Vision 2.0" — in a letter to City Council in March 2025. Resources that could transform the Eastside flow elsewhere instead. The civic league is organized, vocal, and right. What has been missing is a vision concrete enough to demand.

Ward 4 is a majority-Black ward. The Eastside has absorbed the consequences of decisions made elsewhere for decades — the freeway that bisected Church Street, the "urban renewal" that cleared Black-owned commercial corridors, the investment that consistently flowed downtown or to wealthier districts while the Eastside received management rather than resources. "Downtown Tunnel Vision 2.0" is not rhetorical — it names a documented, multigenerational pattern. The flooding that threatens the Eastside's lower-elevation streets disproportionately affects Black residents who had less capacity to invest in flood mitigation and less political leverage to demand it. The highest ground in Norfolk sits in Ward 4. What gets built on it — and who owns it — is a racial equity question as much as a planning question.

The one rule that changes everything

The city doesn't
sell the land.

That's it. The city transfers the land to a Community Land Trust — a resident-governed, nonprofit entity that holds it permanently under ground lease. Buildings go up. People move in. Businesses open. The neighborhood grows and becomes something worth living in.

But the land never goes to market. No future city council can sell it. No developer can buy it. When the neighborhood succeeds — and it will — the rising land value flows back into the commons instead of out to shareholders. The condition that destroys every other great neighborhood is structurally prevented here.

Because land is removed from the financing equation, construction costs fall 25–40% versus conventional development. That gap is closed by federal programs — LIHTC tax credit equity, Virginia Housing construction loans, CDFI impact debt — specifically designed for this. The result is a neighborhood that works financially across every income band, simultaneously, without ongoing city subsidy.

Who controls the CLT — and how

Urban renewal worked because government held eminent domain — the power to take land without community consent and redistribute it at will. The CLT is the structural inverse: the community holds the land, the city cannot reclaim it, and governance requires resident consent. This is not a byproduct of the financial model. It is the design. The CLT board is majority tenant and community-elected — not appointed by the city. Residents vote for representation. Major decisions require community approval. The city contributes the land via ground lease and then steps back; it has no ongoing appointment power. This is the Champlain Housing Trust model, which has governed more than 3,200 homes in northwest Vermont across over 40 years and multiple hostile administrations without political capture. The structure is designed to be durable — including when the politics change.

This isn't a theory.
It has a track record.

Across ownership, cooperative enterprise, and climate resilience — each piece has been done. The question is whether Norfolk will do all three together, before someone else decides what this land is for.

Champlain Housing Trust
Burlington, Vermont
Over 40 years. More than 3,200 permanently affordable homes. CLT condos resold at affordable prices through five ownership cycles — the same below-market price passes to each new buyer. Majority-tenant board, community-elected. Founded in 1984 with city support; now the largest CLT in the US. The governance has survived multiple hostile administrations. The structure holds.
Mondragón Cooperative
Basque Country, Spain
Started in 1956 with a technical school and five students. Now ~70,000 workers across over 80 cooperatives, with an internal credit union that finances cooperative formation from within. Education first, cooperative ownership second, internal capital third. The pattern takes a generation. It compounds. Eastside Commons' cooperative enterprise layer follows this logic directly.
Rotterdam & Philadelphia Green Infrastructure
Netherlands · Pennsylvania
Rotterdam's water squares and green corridors made the city a global model for flood resilience — and increased adjacent property values measurably. Philadelphia's Green City, Clean Waters program has reduced combined sewer overflows by more than 3 billion gallons annually while generating economic co-benefits citywide. The sponge park at Military Circle follows this playbook — and generates the data licensing revenue that funds the community endowment.
East Beach · Harbor Walk · Broad Creek
Norfolk, Virginia
Three large mixed-use redevelopments in Norfolk, all approved via Planned Development (PD) rezoning — one council vote, one master plan, all subsequent development governed without further Council action. The city has done this before, for projects with far weaker public interest rationale. Military Circle at 73 acres is the textbook case for PD designation.
Norfolk right now

The city's capital plan:
$72M for downtown. $0 for Military Circle.

Norfolk allocated $72 million over five years to demolish MacArthur Center downtown. Military Circle — Ward 4, the Eastside — got one sentence: "The City is working to expedite demolition." No dollars. No timeline. Residents dressed in black and filled council chambers. The Eastside Civic League called it a broken promise. Three active city projects show what good intentions without ownership produce — and what Eastside Commons is built to outlast.

Kindred — St. Paul's Transformation
Downtown adjacent · HUD Choice Neighborhoods grant · Brinshore Development

192 units delivered. A third building — Kinship at Kindred — under construction, expected December 2026. Mixed-income design, flood mitigation baked in, Church Street revival as the commercial spine. The right goals.

The constraint: Brinshore is the master developer. The land transfers to a private entity. Residents are tenants of an institution, not stakeholders in land. When Brinshore's interest ends, the structure doesn't hold itself.

Broad Creek · Moton Circle
Ward 6 · ~$30M · NRHA RAD conversion · Construction June 2026

88 apartments renovated via the federal Rental Assistance Demonstration program — converting aging public housing to Section 8, unlocking tax credit financing for significant upgrades. A genuine improvement in living conditions for current residents.

The constraint: RAD conversion preserves the landlord-tenant structure. NRHA remains the owner. The renovation is real; the ownership doesn't change. Residents gain better housing, not a stake in it.

Tide Extension to Military Circle
Hampton Roads Transit · 2.2 miles · 2 stops · Construction target 2027–28

HRT's recommended Phase 1: extend The Tide from Newtown Road to Sentara Leigh Hospital and Military Circle — two new stops, 2.2 additional miles. The city's own economic development director called Military Circle "high ground in Norfolk." HRT explicitly designed the timing to align with redevelopment of this site.

The implication: transit-adjacent density at Military Circle is coming regardless of what gets built. The question is who captures that value when it arrives — a private developer, or the community that governs the land.

What these three projects share — and what's missing

Norfolk is demonstrably capable of executing complex, mixed-income, federally-financed redevelopment. The city knows how to use HUD grants, tax credits, and master plans. It has done it multiple times, in multiple neighborhoods, with real results.

What none of these projects do is transfer ownership. In each case, residents are beneficiaries of institutional decisions — better housing, renovated units, more transit access — but not owners of the asset their presence creates value in. When the political winds shift or the master developer's interest changes, the structure depends on institutional goodwill rather than legal architecture.

Eastside Commons is the proof of concept for a different approach: same federal tools, same mixed-income design, same flood resilience — but with community land ownership as the load-bearing element. If it works here, the model is available to every future redevelopment in Norfolk. Broad Creek's next phase. Young Terrace. Wherever the city holds land and faces the same question: who benefits when this place becomes worth living in?
