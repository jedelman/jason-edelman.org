You know a neighborhood
after it's already gone.

You know the one. Maybe you lived there before it changed, or you discovered it just in time, or someone told you what it used to be. The cheap rents that let artists and immigrants and small restaurants take root. The friction of different people doing different things in the same place. The feeling that something real was happening, unrehearsed.

Then it worked. People noticed. The rents went up. The original residents got pushed out — to somewhere cheaper, somewhere farther, somewhere with fewer options. The coffee shops that replaced the laundromats were nicer, in a way that had nothing to do with the people who made it worth noticing in the first place.

This is not a new story. It has happened to every great urban neighborhood in America. The conditions that generate vitality — cheap space, mixed uses, density of people doing different things — are destroyed by the success they produce. We've never figured out how to stop it.

In Norfolk, Virginia, there is a chance to try.

The opportunity

The city of Norfolk owns
the land already.

In 2022, Norfolk's Economic Development Authority acquired the Military Circle Mall site — 73 acres at the I-264/I-64 nexus, in Ward 4, at the highest elevation in a city sinking 4–5mm per year into the Chesapeake — for a nominal sum. The land has been in public hands ever since.

In most cities, the reason affordable housing is nearly impossible to build is simple: land costs a fortune. 25 to 40 percent of any project's total cost is just the ground beneath it. Here, the city already holds the asset. The hardest part — acquiring the land without debt — is already done.

$0 Land acquisition cost under the commons model. The city already owns it. The subsidy already exists. It's called public land.

Since the acquisition: three developer proposals — Pharrell Williams, Emmitt Smith, Bruce Thompson — each fell apart, for reasons that likely include financing gaps, post-COVID construction cost spikes, and the challenge of structuring a large mixed-use deal in a market that doesn't have many comparable transactions. The city's current placeholder is a library and a fitness center. Not a plan — a signal that no one has yet decided what this land is for.

The Eastside Civic League named the pattern: "Downtown Tunnel Vision 2.0." Resources that could transform the Eastside flow toward downtown instead. The civic league is organized, vocal, and right. What's been missing is a vision concrete enough to demand.

"The highest ground in a flooding city should be where people live — under community control, permanently — not where a mall waits out a lease."

The one rule that changes everything

The city doesn't
sell the land.

That's it. That's the whole structural innovation. The city transfers the land to a Community Land Trust — a resident-governed, nonprofit entity that holds it permanently under ground lease. Buildings go up. People move in. Businesses open. The neighborhood grows and changes and becomes something worth living in.

But the land never goes to market. No future city council can sell it. No developer can buy it. When the neighborhood succeeds — and it will — the rising land value flows back into the commons instead of out to shareholders. The condition that destroys every other great neighborhood is structurally prevented here.

Because land is removed from the capital stack, construction costs fall 25–40% versus conventional development. That gap — closed by federal programs (LIHTC tax credit equity, Virginia Housing construction loans, CDFI impact debt) specifically designed for exactly this — is what makes the numbers work across every income band simultaneously.

This is not a new idea. The Champlain Housing Trust in Burlington, Vermont has done it for 30 years. 2,500 permanently affordable homes, resold at affordable prices through five ownership cycles. The model has a track record. What it needs is the right site and the political will.

This is for everyone.
And that's the point.

A neighborhood that works for one income band is as monocultural as a suburb. Vitality requires mixing. The structure makes mixing possible without displacement.

The climate-aware buyer
You understand what coastal real estate looks like in 20 years.
Military Circle is 8–12 feet elevation. Flood Zone X — the lowest risk classification. Norfolk sinks 4–5mm per year. The neighborhoods around this site will face increasing flood events over the next two decades. This site will not. Climate-safe real estate in a coastal region is going to be genuinely scarce. The commons structure means the value of that resilience stays in the community.
"This is the highest ground. And I'd actually own it."

LIHTC 9% equity (~$68M site-wide), VHDA construction financing, CDFI impact debt at patient rates. The structure holds

This isn't a theory.
It has a track record.

Across ownership, cooperative enterprise, and climate resilience — each piece has been done. What hasn't been done is all three, at this scale, at once.

Champlain Housing Trust
Burlington, Vermont
30 years. 2,500 permanently affordable homes. CLT condos resold at affordable prices through five ownership cycles. The gold standard for the land trust model. Founded in 1984 with city support, now the largest community land trust in the US. Proves the structure works — and stays.
Mondragón Cooperative Corporation
Basque Country, Spain
Started in 1956 with a technical school and 5 students. Now 80,000 worker-owners across 100 cooperatives, with an internal credit union that finances cooperative formation from within the network. The pattern: education → skills → cooperative ownership → internal capital → more cooperatives. The commercial layer of Eastside Commons follows this logic.
Wuhan Sponge City Program
Wuhan, China
389 projects across 38.5 km². Flood events reduced 10×. Urban heat reduced 3°C. Adjacent land values up 2×. The research station model enables data licensing. Norfolk is a direct coastal analog — the same logic applies, with the added fact that the sponge park's revenue funds the community endowment that makes everything else permanent.
East Beach · Harbor Walk · Broad Creek
Norfolk, Virginia
Three large mixed-use redevelopments in Norfolk itself, all approved via Planned Development (PD) rezoning — one council vote, master plan governs all subsequent development. The city knows how to do this. The vehicle is precedented locally. Military Circle at 73 acres is the textbook case for PD designation.
