Folded Arms

Today we live side by side without knowing one another.Pëtr Kropotkin, Anarchism: Its Philosophy and Ideal, 1898

Steam mills in Poblenou, stevedores on the wharf, bakers asleep on flour sacks. After a bomb at the Liceu opera house in 1893, the police arrested 415 people; the bomber said he had acted alone. The papers call every meeting, every strike and every mutual-aid society anarchy. Here it means what Kropotkin meant: people running their own lives. Talk to the workers and listen. Run errands across the city for the ones who need a hand first. Win a majority, march on the foreman, and the shop joins the federation.

The story follows Roser, a mill girl, through the fight for the eight-hour day, with the real history after every chapter. Free play drops you in the city to organize six shops on your own.

The foreman's gaze is the danger. The street is public; the yard is theirs. The Ateneu Obrer and every organized yard are safe: your comrades close ranks around you. The red beacon marks your next job.

General Strike

The city stopped. Then it kept going: the bread was baked, the trams ran on the drivers' timetable, the wharf was unloaded in turns, and the looms in Poblenou went quiet until the fines were gone. The Diari de l'Ordre called it anarchy the whole time, until the printers stopped setting it.

Only, instead of demanding that those social customs should be maintained through the authority of a few, it demands it from the continued action of all.Pëtr Kropotkin, Anarchism: Its Philosophy and Ideal, 1898

The title is Kropotkin's too: strikes, he wrote in the same lecture, were once "a war of folded arms." Every shop, foreman and worker in the game is invented.
