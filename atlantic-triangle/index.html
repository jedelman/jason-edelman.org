Atlantic Triangle

A connected-history timeline · England · the Gold Coast (Ghana) · America

A friend asked whether anything existed that visualized how a country's history — its geography, its resources, the outside powers that came for both — shapes its national psychology and its position in the world over time. Nothing quite like that turned up. This is a proof of concept, not the answer: one connected cluster of three places whose histories are inseparable from each other, laid out on a shared timeline.

England, the Gold Coast, and colonial/early America are not three parallel stories — they're one story told from three vantage points. English ships built forts on the Gold Coast to extract gold, then people; the people taken were forced into the plantation economies of English colonies in America; the wealth those colonies generated flowed back to England. The timeline below tracks each place on its own axis, with shaded bands marking the periods where their histories were most directly entangled.

Scope: this covers one cluster as a proof of concept — not "every country," which was the original, much larger ask. A second cluster (Mexico/Spain was suggested) is a natural next step if this format holds up. National borders shifted constantly over this span (the Ashanti Empire and Gold Coast are not the same polity as modern Ghana, no more than colonial Virginia is the modern United States); the tracks below follow the geographic region, not a fixed nation-state.

Reading the throughline

England / Britain

England's trajectory from a minor North Atlantic kingdom to a global empire runs directly through state-chartered extraction — the Royal African Company, the East India Company's forebears, the colonization of North America. Losing the American colonies in 1783 didn't end the model; it redirected it toward Africa and Asia. Losing the Gold Coast in 1957, the first African colony to gain independence, marked the start of the empire's unwinding rather than an isolated event.

Gold Coast / Ghana

The region's name in European accounts — "the Gold Coast" — describes what it was understood to be worth to outsiders, not what its people called it. The Ashanti Empire built real regional power on control of gold, then found that same coastline turned into the busiest slaving corridor in West Africa as European demand shifted from metal to people. Ghana's 1957 independence, the first in sub-Saharan Africa, was framed by Nkrumah explicitly as incomplete until linked to the liberation of the whole continent.

Colonial America / United States

Jamestown and the arrival of the first enslaved Africans at Point Comfort happen twelve years apart, and the country's founding mythology and its founding contradiction are nearly the same age. The line from 1619 to the Civil War to the civil rights movement is continuous, not a story of a settled past — and it runs directly back through the Atlantic to the coast the English called Gold.
