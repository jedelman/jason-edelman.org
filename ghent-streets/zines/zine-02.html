Zine No. 2

A Tale of
Two Streets

Colonial Avenue vs. Colley Avenue. Same city. Same era. Same kind of neighborhood. The difference is three letters: P — C — O.

Ghent Streets · Norfolk, Virginia · HC-G2 · Free to print and share

1

Two streets. One city. One alive, one dead. Why?

Take a walk down Colonial Avenue in Ghent. Beautiful houses. Mature trees. Short blocks. Sidewalks wide enough for two people to pass comfortably. It has every physical feature of a neighborhood street that should be teeming with life. At 7pm on a Friday, it is quiet. Not peaceful quiet — empty quiet. The lights are on inside the houses and the sidewalk belongs to no one.

Now walk five minutes to Colley Avenue. Same city. Same era of construction. Same dense residential neighborhood, with a commercial corridor running through it. Colley on a Friday evening is a different world: restaurants full, bars lit up, people sitting outside, foot traffic, the low-grade noise of a place that is actually being used.

The buildings and the people didn't make this difference. The zoning code made this difference. And the zoning code can be changed.

2

What does Colley have that Colonial doesn't?

Colley Avenue has a Pedestrian Commercial Overlay — a PCO. It's an addition to the base zoning that says: on this street, restaurants, shops and personal services are allowed as long as they meet the overlay's performance standards. You want to open a café? Open a café. A bookstore? A bookstore. A permit comes in only where the standards call for one. You find a space, you sign a lease, you open.

Colonial Avenue is in HC-G2. There is no PCO. In HC-G2's column of the use table, the cells for retail, restaurants, and personal services are blank, and a blank cell means prohibited. There's no permit to apply for and no fee that buys an exception. The only way through is for City Council to change the table.

That's the whole difference: a three-letter designation in a table in the city code.

Colonial Avenue · HC-G2

No commercial life by right

  • Retail: prohibited
  • Restaurant: prohibited
  • Café: prohibited
  • Personal services: prohibited
  • No permit to apply for, at any price
  • Only a Council vote to amend the table can change it
  • Result: 9 commercial parcels out of 420
Colley Avenue · PCO

Commercial life by right

  • Retail: permitted, no application required
  • Restaurant: permitted, no application required
  • Café: permitted, no application required
  • Personal services: permitted, no application required
  • Performance standards manage scale and hours
  • ARB reviews exterior changes as always
  • Result: a living street
3

But didn't the PCO change Colley's character?

Drive down Colley and look at it. The Craftsman bungalows behind the storefronts are still there, and so is the tree canopy. The front porches, the corner houses, and the neighborhood feel are all intact. Nobody tore down Victorian houses to build a strip mall because the PCO was adopted.

What changed is that the ground floors of buildings along the commercial corridor were activated. The storefronts that were previously vacant or used for storage became restaurants and shops. The parking lots didn't multiply, and the traffic didn't transform. The neighborhood absorbed its commercial life the way dense neighborhoods are supposed to, at human scale and one business at a time.

That's what would happen on Colonial. The bones are there, the potential foot traffic is there, and so are the residents who want to walk somewhere. The only missing piece is legal permission.

The PCO on Colley didn't change what Colley looks like. It changed what Colley is: a street people actually use.
4

What about the historic designation? Doesn't that protect Ghent from commercial overdevelopment?

HC-G2 has real architectural protections that are not touched by this proposal. The Architectural Review Board reviews any exterior change to a building — paint colors, window replacements, additions, signage, awnings. Those standards stay. Any business that opens on Colonial will still need ARB approval for anything that alters the building's appearance.

The proposal changes the use classification: whether a business can operate inside an existing building without physically altering it. A coffee shop in an existing ground-floor space changes nothing about the historic fabric.

Norfolk's own historic district design guidelines already cover storefronts, awnings and signs, because the city's historic districts include commercial buildings. The ARB governs what buildings look like, and zoning governs what they're allowed to be.

5

Does street activation make things safer for everyone, including the most vulnerable people?

Urban planners have argued since Jane Jacobs that streets with foot traffic, business hours, and people coming and going are safer for everyone on them, including people without stable housing. Vacant commercial frontage with no eyes on the street is more dangerous than an active one, for the reasons Jacobs gave in 1961: informal surveillance, social presence, and natural foot traffic reduce the risk of harm.

This proposal doesn't address the need for housing and services for people experiencing homelessness. Those are real needs that require their own policy responses. It does address the design of the street: a street with a coffee shop and a bookstore and regular foot traffic is a safer and more connected environment than a blank residential wall, for everyone who uses it.

6

So what are we asking the city to do?

We're asking the City of Norfolk to amend Table 3.6.11 of the Zoning Ordinance to allow neighborhood-scale retail uses by right on ground floors of parcels with street frontage. That's the table that governs what's permitted in HC-G2. It isn't a new overlay or a new district, just a change to a table in a document that already exists.

The language already exists in the PCO sections of the code. The process is the normal text amendment process: ARB recommendation, Planning Commission hearing, City Council vote. We are asking for nothing extraordinary, only for Norfolk to let HC-G2's ground floors do what Colley Avenue's already do.

That's it: one change to one table, and a neighborhood whose streets finally work the way they were designed to.

Same city. Same tools. Norfolk already knows how to do this.
We're just asking them to do it here.

What You Can Do

Show up. When the amendment moves forward, the ARB will review it and the Planning Commission will hold a public hearing. Your presence matters more than you think.

Write a letter. Contact your City Council members (Ward 2 and Superward 6) and tell them what you want on your street. One paragraph. Your name and address. Done.

Share this. Print more copies of this zine. Leave them at the library, the coffee shop you had to drive to, your neighbor's porch. The more people who understand this, the harder it is to ignore.
