Zine No. 3

Your Neighborhood
Is Worth More
Than This

What the zoning code costs Ghent: in city revenue, in what you can walk to, and maybe in property value, though the research there is mixed.

Ghent Streets · Norfolk, Virginia · HC-G2 · Free to print and share

1

The average residential parcel in Ghent is assessed at about $456,000. What does zoning have to do with it?

Across Ghent's four planning districts (61–64), the average residential parcel, condos included, is assessed at about $456,000. That's not a bad number. Ghent is an attractive, historically significant, walkable neighborhood in the heart of Norfolk.

But core Ghent has 945 parcels, and only 13 of them (1.4%) are commercial. Along Colley Avenue, whose overlay blocks are a five-minute walk away, 106 of 229 parcels are commercial: 46%. Same city.

That gap is a zoning outcome, not a market outcome. What it costs shows up most clearly in city revenue. What it does to your own assessment is a harder question, and the next page is straight with you about it.

Source: Norfolk FY2026 Property Assessment dataset (data.norfolk.gov, dataset m5ya-5grb), filtered to Planning District 64 and street_name='Colley'; residential average across PD 61–64.

2

Does having commercial activity nearby actually make residential property worth more?

Sometimes, a little. The research is mixed.

The best-matched study looked at home sales in and around Seattle from 1989 to 2003. In the older, grid-street neighborhoods, homes a short walk from shops (about 235 to 1,260 feet) sold for slightly more, about $2,000 at the peak, while homes right next door to the shops sold for less. In the car-oriented suburbs, distance to shops made no measurable difference. Other studies find buyers paying a little more for walkable, mixed-use neighborhoods, and some find nothing at all.

Norfolk's own numbers don't show a premium either. From 2021 to 2026, residential assessments along Colley Avenue rose 27.6%; across the whole city they rose 47.7%.

So we won't promise your house will be worth more. The case for the amendment is what you could walk to, and what the city would collect.

Matthews, "Retail Proximity and Residential Values or Do Nearby Stores Really Run Down Property Values?", Georgia State University, Working Paper 07-21 (2007). Norfolk FY2021 and FY2026 assessment datasets (8bfx-a5g8, m5ya-5grb), residential parcels. Full discussion in Policy Brief No. 2, Section IV-A.

What you can count on is the coffee you could walk to. The city's side, by our model: about $227,000 a year in taxes from fifteen businesses, some of it spending that moves from elsewhere in town.

If commercial properties are worth more, why aren't Colonial Avenue's commercial parcels worth more?

On Colley Avenue, commercial parcels are assessed at 2.7 to 3 times the value of residential parcels on the same street. That commercial premium is what you'd expect in a functioning mixed-use neighborhood: businesses in ground-floor spaces generate income, and income-producing property is worth more than residential-only property.

Now look at Colonial Avenue. The five commercial parcels that exist there have a median assessed value of $495,000. The residential parcels on the same street have a median assessed value of $487,000. That's a wash. Commercial and residential are worth essentially the same thing on Colonial.

What does this cost the city, and what does that mean for your services?

Ghent sits on $3.36 billion in total assessed real estate value across its four planning districts. That's what the city has to work with as a tax base. Yet less than 3% of those parcels are commercial, and commercial is the use type that generates direct sales tax, business license tax, and meals tax on top of the property tax that every parcel pays.

Our model, built on Norfolk's tax rates, projects that 15 new ground-floor businesses in HC-G2 would bring the city about $227,000 a year in taxes, mostly from the 6.5% meals tax on food and beverage sales, and 40 would bring about $602,000. Some of that spending would simply move from Colley or Granby, where it's already taxed, so the net gain is smaller. Over a decade, the gross total reaches at most $1.7 million to $4.4 million.

That revenue pays for street maintenance, parks, and code enforcement, the services that make a neighborhood worth living in. Ghent's empty ground floors are more than an aesthetic problem. They're revenue the city has chosen to forgo.

5

So why doesn't someone just open a café and prove the demand exists?

Because they can't. The use is prohibited.

In HC-G2, a café isn't something you can apply for. There's no permit, no fee, and no hearing that gets one open on Colonial. The only route is a change to the ordinance itself: a text amendment adopted by City Council.

The person who wants to open a coffee shop in the ground floor of a Colonial Avenue Victorian can't open until the city rewrites its code, so the café opens somewhere else, or not at all. The demand isn't absent. The law gives it nowhere to land.

Ghent residents spend their money on food and retail on Colley, on Granby, and on 21st Street. Economists call that revealed preference. The demand exists and is expressed elsewhere, because elsewhere the zoning lets a bookstore open.

Source: Norfolk Zoning Ordinance, Table 3.6.11, whose key reads "blank cell = prohibited."

6

What does the fix look like?

A single change to a table in the Norfolk Zoning Ordinance. Table 3.6.11 governs what uses are permitted in HC-G2. The proposal is to change the cells for neighborhood-scale retail from blank (prohibited) to P: permitted by right. That covers small restaurants, cafés, bookstores, salons, dry cleaners, and small fitness studios.

By right means: if you have a space that meets the performance standards (ground floor only, size caps, hours limits, ARB review for any exterior changes), you can open. No hearing and no Council vote for each shop. You find a space, you sign a lease, you open. That's the model Colley Avenue's overlay already uses.

The historic protections don't change. The Architectural Review Board still reviews any exterior modification: paint, signage, windows, awnings. What changes is whether a business is allowed to exist inside an existing building. The amendment separates the question of what buildings look like from the question of what they're allowed to be used for.

What can you do?

The path to change runs through a public hearing at the ARB, a Planning Commission vote, and City Council. All of those bodies respond to constituent presence. Here's how to help:

  • Show up at hearings. When the amendment moves forward, there will be public hearings. Planners and council members count the bodies in the room.
  • Write your council member. Ghent's HC-G2 is in Ward 2 and Superward 6. Send a short, personal email about what you want on your street: one paragraph, with your name and address. It's worth more than any policy brief.
  • Talk to your neighbors. Print this zine. Leave it on porches. The more people who understand that this is a zoning problem with a known solution, the harder it is to ignore.
  • If you're a property owner with a ground-floor space that could be activated: your name and your space matter. Contact the campaign.
