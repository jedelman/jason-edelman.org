Dead Streets and Displacement: The Link No One Talks About

We think unhoused people concentrate on Ghent streets for structural reasons, chief among them the absence of anything open and of the informal activity that comes with it. This campaign addresses that part of the picture, without policing.

Urban Safety · Street Activation Homelessness & Land Use 2025

When residents of Ghent complain about unhoused individuals on their streets, the conversation usually goes one of two ways. It either becomes a discussion about social services (shelter beds, outreach workers, mental health resources, all of which matter) or it becomes, uncomfortably, a conversation about enforcement: more police, more citations, more pressure to move on.

Zoning almost never comes up.

Which is striking, because urban planners have spent decades arguing that the physical and regulatory conditions of streets shape who uses them, how, and how safe they are for everyone present.

Jane Jacobs and eyes on the street

Jane Jacobs wrote about "eyes on the street" in 1961. The idea is simple: streets are safe when they are used, and they are used when there are things to do on them at multiple hours of the day. A café generates foot traffic at 7am, a lunch spot at noon, a bar at 10pm, a dry cleaner on Saturday morning. Each of these uses extends the hours when a street has people on it. Nobody planned that; commerce produces the movement on its own.

A residential-only street, particularly one where residents move between cars and houses without lingering, has a much narrower window of natural activity: early morning, early evening, a few dog walks. The street is vacant for most of its hours.

People who have nowhere else to be concentrate on vacant urban streets, because everyone else has left or never had a reason to come. That isn't a moral judgment about unhoused people; it's a description of how space works when it is underused.

"We have designed the vacancy. The vacancy produces the conditions we don't like. The solution is not more enforcement of the vacancy — it's filling it."

The specific mechanism in HC-G2

HC-G2's prohibition on commercial uses removes the main way urban streets fill up on their own. There is no coffee shop opening at 6:30am with a light on, no lunch counter bringing people out at midday, no evening spot giving anyone a reason to walk past the same block twice.

The result is what you see: blocks that are architecturally beautiful, densely populated and within walking distance of many people's daily needs, and still dead at the sidewalk level for most of the day. The people who fill that vacancy aren't there out of spite; they're there because the space is, structurally, empty.

Ghent isn't unusual in this. The pattern is documented in urban neighborhoods where use restrictions have removed the commercial layer that would otherwise activate the streetscape, whether those restrictions come from historic zoning, residential-only designations or suburban-style land use planning applied to dense urban fabric.

Activated streets

Here is what we expect when ground-floor commercial uses come to a street that had none, and what Jacobs described on the streets she studied. Foot traffic increases throughout the day. Informal social ties form between business owners and regular customers. Eyes are on the street at more hours. The zone between buildings and sidewalks, the public-private threshold, becomes occupied and informally watched.

Not every study agrees. Sampson and Raudenbush's block-by-block observation of Chicago neighborhoods found that "neighborhoods where land use is mixed tend to have higher levels of physical and social disorder." More activity on a street brings more of everything, which is why the amendment comes with hours, size and signage limits.

These changes do not eliminate homelessness, and they do not provide housing or mental health services. The people who are currently sleeping in Ghent's parks and sitting on its stoops do not disappear when a café opens next door. But the quality of the street changes for everyone, including them. A street with active uses has more informal support: a place to get water, a bathroom, a bench in front of a business where presence is normal rather than anomalous.

Street activation also reduces concentration, which matters more. When streets have mixed uses, no single block or stretch becomes the default location for people with nowhere else to go. The activity is spread out, and the streets are shared rather than sorted, de facto, by who has alternatives and who doesn't.

The enforcement alternative

The instinct is to respond to street-level discomfort with enforcement: moving people along, issuing citations for loitering or camping, increasing police presence. That instinct is understandable. We don't think it produces the outcome residents say they want.

Enforcement moves people; it does not reduce the number of people or their need for somewhere to be. The emptiness that made Ghent's streets the default location remains. A sweep leaves in place the underlying cause, the absence of competing uses for the space, so the conditions that drew people there remain too.

Enforcement on a commercially dead street also sets residents and unhoused individuals against each other as adversaries competing for the same dead space. Commercial activation changes that. On a street with a lunch spot and a yoga studio and a bookstore, housed residents, customers, business owners and people experiencing homelessness are all present in the same space, at the same time, with legitimate reasons to be there. That isn't utopia, just a street that works.

Our proposal and its limits

This campaign is not about homelessness directly. We are not a social services organization and we are not proposing housing policy. We are proposing a zoning text amendment that will permit neighborhood-scale commercial uses on ground floors in HC-G2.

But that amendment matters beyond the obvious quality-of-life benefits for residents who want a coffee shop. The current dead-street condition in Ghent is a spatial equity problem. It concentrates vulnerability on a set of blocks that the zoning code has stripped of the mechanisms that would otherwise distribute and reduce that concentration.

We are asking for the right to fill the vacancy with something other than nothing. The problem is the vacancy, not the people who currently occupy it.

REFERENCES: Jane Jacobs, The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961), Chapter 2: "The Uses of Sidewalks: Safety"; Robert J. Sampson and Stephen W. Raudenbush, "Systematic Social Observation of Public Spaces," American Journal of Sociology 105(3) (1999), and their National Institute of Justice research brief (2001, ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/186049.pdf).
