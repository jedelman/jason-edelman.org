How Norfolk's Zoning Code Kills Street Life

A close reading of Table 3.6.11, the table in Norfolk's zoning code that governs what can happen on your block, and what its blank cells mean for the streets you walk every day.

HC-G2 · Norfolk Zoning Ordinance § 3.6 Table 3.6.11 2025

There is a table in the Norfolk Zoning Ordinance called Table 3.6.11. It has never been in the news, and most of the people who live inside the district it governs have never heard of it. But if you have ever stood on a Ghent sidewalk at 9pm wondering where everyone is, this table is a large part of the reason.

Table 3.6.11 is the principal use table for Norfolk's Historic and Cultural Conservation districts. It lists every category of land use (residential, commercial, institutional, industrial) and marks each one with a symbol for each HC sub-district. P means the use is permitted by right. C means you can apply for a Conditional Use Permit and maybe get approved. A blank cell means the use is prohibited.

HC-G2 is the Ghent district, the one that covers Colonial Avenue and the surrounding residential blocks. In its column, most commercial uses aren't marked C or made subject to conditions. They are blank.

Blank means prohibited

A blank cell in the use table is a prohibition: that use cannot legally operate on that land. A property owner who wants to convert a ground floor to retail can't get there with a permit, with an appeal or by posting a bond. The use is not available in that district.

For HC-G2, the blanks cover eating and drinking establishments of all types, retail sales of virtually all kinds, personal services (hair salons, tailors, dry cleaners), fitness studios, art galleries operating as commercial uses, and professional service offices in street-facing ground-floor positions.

Nine of HC-G2's 420 parcels are assessed as commercial: the 7-Eleven, a restaurant, two shops, two offices, one other commercial building and two parking lots. They can stay, but under today's table no new business in those categories could open.

"A blank cell is not a 'no, not here.' It's a 'no, not anywhere, not ever, not without amending the ordinance itself.' That's a different kind of prohibition."

The C column

Only three uses in HC-G2 are marked C: boarding schools, bed-and-breakfasts and inns. For those, a C means you can apply for a Conditional Use Permit.

Under Norfolk's zoning procedures, a CUP application involves: a completed application with site plan and use description; a city application fee of $1,080 (Norfolk Planning Fee Schedule, effective July 1, 2024), plus legal and planning consultant costs that typically range from $2,000–$8,000 for a complete CUP application in a historic district; a referral to the Architectural Review Board for a recommendation (because this is a historic district); a public hearing before the City Planning Commission with legally required notice in The Virginian-Pilot; a Planning Commission vote to recommend approval or denial; and a final vote by City Council at a subsequent public hearing.

From application to Council vote takes approximately four to eight months under normal circumstances, and the outcome is not guaranteed.

A neighborhood coffee shop or a yoga studio doesn't get even that. Their cells are blank, so there is no application to file. Small business owners who want to serve Ghent locate somewhere the zoning allows them: Colley Avenue, 21st Street, Granby Street.

A design choice

The current Norfolk Zoning Ordinance was adopted in 2018 and replaced a 1950-era code. The HC districts were written to emphasize preservation of residential character above all else. That instinct is defensible: Ghent's housing stock is worth protecting.

But the drafters conflated two distinct things: the physical character of structures (which historic preservation legitimately protects) and the commercial use of ground floors (which has nothing to do with whether a building looks like it belongs in 1905). A café in an existing building changes neither the facade nor the massing nor the materials. The ARB exists to protect those things, and it has no authority to stop a change of use absent an exterior alteration. The use table stops it instead, bluntly and comprehensively.

The result is a district frozen commercially but not architecturally, since buildings can be renovated, modernized, even badly altered. The ground floors of Ghent are legally stranded as residential-only, and every indicator of urban health suffers for it: walkability, social cohesion, small business formation, tax base.

Colley Avenue

Walk five minutes from the middle of the district and you are on Colley Avenue, which has a PCO (Pedestrian Commercial Overlay) designation layered on top of its residential base zoning. That overlay allows restaurants, shops, personal services and offices on the ground floor: offices by right, the rest by right when they meet the overlay's performance standards, with a conditional use permit only where the standards call for one. A business that meets them opens with a zoning certificate and a business license.

The result is visible: 106 of Colley Avenue's 229 parcels are commercial. It has the kind of street life that urbanists write about and economists study and city councils point to when they want to show that Norfolk is a good place to do business.

HC-G2 has the same density as the blocks behind Colley Avenue, with better architecture, shorter blocks and more residential population within walking distance. It doesn't have the PCO, and so it doesn't have the street life.

Amending Table 3.6.11

The fix isn't complicated: amend Table 3.6.11 to change specific blank cells to P, permitted by right, for a defined list of neighborhood-scale commercial uses on ground floors. That's a text amendment. It doesn't rezone anything, remove the ARB or override the architectural protections that make HC designation meaningful.

It just fills in the blanks.

The specific language is in Policy Brief No. 1, along with the performance standards that would accompany it: hours, size limits, activation requirements. Norfolk has already tested the idea. It did this on Colley Avenue and on 21st Street, and it has done it on every pedestrian commercial corridor that works. The only question is whether the political will exists to do it in Ghent.

We think it does. That's what this campaign is for.
